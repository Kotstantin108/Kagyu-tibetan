# Theory texts of the lessons, English. Examples only from the sentences of the course.

## lesson-01

Classical Tibetan has no articles, no case endings and no agreement. A word never changes its shape; the meaning of a phrase comes from the order of the words and from small particles placed after them. So the first skill of the course is a single one: to see where a noun phrase ends and in what order its words stand.

The order is fixed: noun, then adjective, then number, then demonstrative, then plural particle. The adjective always follows its noun, the reverse of English: དགོན་པ་དམར་པོ་ "red monastery", ཁང་པ་རྙིང་པ་ "old house". The demonstratives འདི་ "this" and དེ་ "that" also follow: བླ་མ་འདི་ "this lama", དགོན་པ་དཀར་པོ་དེ་ "that white monastery". The full chain: རྟ་དཀར་པོ་ལྔ་འདི་དག་ཐམས་ཅད་ "all these five white horses". Used alone as a pronoun, the demonstrative opens the sentence, and the linking verb ཡིན་ "is" closes it: འདི་བླ་མ་ཡིན། "This is a guru."

A plural is not obligatory. When it is needed, རྣམས་ "plural particle" or དག་ "plural particle" closes the whole phrase: དེ་རྣམས་ "those", དགོན་པ་དམར་པོ་འདི་དག་ "these red monasteries". Where English says "a" or "some", Tibetan puts an indefinite particle after the phrase; it is written ཅིག, ཞིག or ཤིག according to the last letter of the syllable before it: ལམ་ཞིག་ "a path", སངས་རྒྱས་ཤིག་ "a buddha". After a number, པོ་ "group of" presents the members taken together: གཉིས་པོ་ "the (group of) two".

The particle པ་ "one associated with" turns a word into the person connected with it: ཆོས་ "Dharma", ཆོས་པ་ "Dharma practitioner"; རྟ་ "horse", རྟ་པ་ "horse rider". The endings མ་ "feminine ending" and མོ་ "feminine ending" can mark the feminine: རྣལ་འབྱོར་མ་ "yoginī", རྒྱལ་མོ་ "queen".

The members of a list are joined by དང་ "and": དགེ་བ་དང་མི་དགེ་བ་ "virtue and non-virtue", ཐབས་དང་ཤེས་རབ་ "method and wisdom". A list often closes with a number totalling its members, where English adds nothing: སངས་རྒྱས་དང་ཆོས་དང་དགེ་འདུན་གསུམ་ "Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha". An open list ends with ལ་སོགས་པ་ "and so forth", often shortened to སོགས་ "etc.": སྦྱིན་པ་དང་ཚུལ་ཁྲིམས་དང་བཟོད་པ་ལ་སོགས་པ་ "generosity, discipline, patience, and so forth".

After words of possession, separation and relation, the same དང་ "and" means "with" or "from": དཔལ་དང་ལྡན་པ་ "endowed with glory", སྤྲོས་པ་དང་བྲལ་བ་ "free from mental constructs", བླ་མ་དང་འབྲེལ་བ་ "associated with the guru".

## lesson-02

The connective particle joins two nouns so that the first describes the second. The formula is A + particle + B, and the meaning is "B of A", so the phrase is read from right to left, the opposite of the English "of"-phrase: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་བསྟན་པ་ "the teachings of the Buddha", བོད་ཀྱི་མི་ "people of Tibet". English puts the possessor first only with the apostrophe, "the Buddha's teachings"; Tibetan always does.

The particle has five written forms, and the choice depends on the last letter of the syllable before it. After ག and ང write གི; after ད, བ and ས write ཀྱི; after ན, མ, ར and ལ write གྱི; after an open syllable, and after a final འ, write འི fused to the syllable: རྒྱལ་མོའི་ཤེས་རབ་ "the wisdom of the queen", བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའི་ཐེག་པ་ "the vehicle of the bodhisattva". In verse the fused འི is often replaced by the separate syllable ཡི་ "of". All five sound alike; only the spelling differs, so the rule is learnt by heart.

The particle refers to the whole phrase before it, a list included: བླ་མ་དང་སློབ་མའི་དགོན་པ་ "the monastery of the guru and the students". When several particles follow one another, keep connecting from right to left: བལ་ཡུལ་གྱི་དགོན་པ་དཀར་པོའི་བླ་མའི་བསྟན་པ་ "the teachings of the guru of the white monastery of Nepal".

Tibetan has no separate possessive pronouns. It builds them from a personal pronoun and the same particle, as English builds "the king's": ང་ "I", ངའི་བླ་མ་ "my guru"; ཁྱོད་ "you", ཁྱོད་ཀྱི་སློབ་མ་བཟང་པོ་ "your excellent disciple"; ཁོང་ "he, she", ཁོང་གི་ང་རྒྱལ་ཆེན་པོ་ "his great pride"; བདག་ཅག་ "we", བདག་ཅག་གི་རྟ་དཀར་པོ་གསུམ་ "our three white horses".

Not every such link is best rendered by "of". བྱང་ཆུབ་ཀྱི་ལམ་ "the path to enlightenment" and བླ་མའི་མཆོད་པ་ "the offerings for the guru" want another preposition. The particle can also put an adjective before its noun: དམ་པའི་ཆོས་ "sacred Dharma", ཟབ་མོའི་ལམ་ "profound path". And it can join two names of the same thing, an apposition: འཆི་བདག་གི་གཤིན་རྗེ་ "the lord of death, Yama". An apposition may also stand without any particle: འཆི་བདག་གཤིན་རྗེ་ "the lord of death, Yama", སངས་རྒྱས་འོད་དཔག་མེད་ "Buddha Amitābha".

Finally ནི་ "as for". It singles out what stands before it, roughly like "as for ..." at the head of an English sentence: ཁོང་ནི་ "as for him, ...", ངའི་ཉོན་མོངས་ནི་ "as for my afflictions, ...". In translation it is most often left out.

## lesson-03

The verb stands last in a Tibetan sentence; subject, objects and adverbials all come before it. This lesson has the three verbs on which almost any text rests: ཡིན་ "is", ཡོད་ "there is", and the ordinary verb of action.

ཡིན་ "is" links a thing with its description, like English "is". The subject comes first, and neither it nor the description takes a particle: དགོན་པ་འདི་དཀར་པོ་ཡིན། "This monastery is white." The subject is often marked by ནི་ "as for": ཁྱོད་ནི་སློབ་མ་གསར་པ་མ་ཡིན། "You are not a new student." The negation puts མ་ "not" before the verb or uses the contracted མིན་ "is not". This is the only verb that is often left out, as in the short statements of doctrine: འདུས་བྱས་ཐམས་ཅད་མི་རྟག་པ། "All conditioned things are impermanent."

ཡོད་ "there is" states that something exists: ཆོས་པ་མང་པོ་ཡོད། "There are many Dharma practitioners." Its negation is a separate verb, མེད་ "there is not". The place takes ལ་ "in, at": བདག་བལ་ཡུལ་ལ་ཡོད། "I am in Nepal." Word order is free with this verb, and placing the place first stresses it. English "to have" uses the same verb: the owner takes ལ་ "to", the thing owned has no particle, literally "to me there is a house": བདག་ལ་ཁང་པ་ཆེན་པོ་ཞིག་ཡོད། "I have a big house."

With verbs of action Tibetan does the opposite of English. English marks nothing and relies on word order; Tibetan marks the agent with the agentive particle and leaves the object bare: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་ཆོས་བསྟན། "The Buddha taught the Dharma." Such verbs are transitive. The particle itself is the subject of the next lesson; for now it is enough to recognise it. After an open syllable it fuses with the word as ས: ང་ "I", ངས་ཀུ་ཤུ་ཟ། "I eat an apple." Verbs of benefit and harm take their object with ལ་ "to": དགྲ་འདིས་ཆོས་པ་ལ་གནོད། "This enemy harms the Dharma practitioner."

Intransitive verbs, whose subject does nothing to anyone, leave the subject bare: ཉི་མ་ཤར། "The sun rose." Verbs of motion behave the same way, and the destination takes ལ་ "to": བདག་བོད་ལ་འགྲོ། "I go to Tibet." Occasionally the one who goes does take the agentive particle: ཨ་མས་དེར་ཀྱང་ཕྱིན། "Mother also went there." Negation stands right before the verb: མ་ "not" with past and imperative forms, མི་ "not" with present and future: གྲྭ་པས་ཆོས་མ་བསྟན། "The monk did not teach the Dharma."

In classical texts a sentence is often closed by a terminating particle: the last letter of the verb is repeated with the vowel o, ཡིན་ "is" + ནོ་ "terminating particle": དགེ་བ་ནི་བདེ་བའི་རྒྱུ་ཡིན་ནོ། "Virtue is the cause of happiness." After an open syllable it is a fused འོ, and the ending པའོ་ "terminating ending", a nominalised verb with that འོ, can close any sentence whatever the verb's last letter: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་ཆོས་བསྟན་པའོ། "The Buddha taught the Dharma." After a final ག the shad is not written: བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་ནི་ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་ལམ་ལ་འཇུག་གོ་ "The bodhisattva enters the path of the Greater Vehicle."

## lesson-04

The agentive particle marks the one who performs the action of a transitive verb. English has no equivalent and leaves it untranslated: ཁོང་གིས་རི་ལ་ལྟའོ། "He looks at the mountains." Its forms follow the rule of the connective particle, with ས added: after ག and ང write གིས; after ད, བ and ས write ཀྱིས; after ན, མ, ར and ལ write གྱིས; after an open syllable a fused ས. In verse it may be a separate syllable, ཡིས་ "agentive particle": བླ་མ་ཡིས་དབང་བཞི་སྐུར་རོ། "The guru bestows the four empowerments." The particle applies to the whole group before it, however long: རྣལ་འབྱོར་པ་དེ་དག་དང་དགོན་པ་དཀར་པོའི་བླ་མ་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱིས་ཆོས་སྟོན། "All those yogins and the gurus of the white monastery teach the Dharma."

The same particle has a second meaning, the instrument, English "with" or "by means of". A sentence may contain both: ངས་ཀུ་ཤུ་ལག་པས་ཟ། "I eat an apple with my hands." A noun or adjective with this particle becomes an adverb, as English adds "-ly": ནན་ཏན་གྱིས་ "persistently", རང་བཞིན་གྱིས་ "naturally". With the verb "to be empty" it marks what something is empty of: ཕུང་པོ་ལྔ་ནི་བདག་གིས་སྟོང་ངོ་། "The five aggregates are empty of self."

Not every verb with an object takes an agent in the agentive. Verbs of emotion leave the subject bare and mark the object with ལ་ "to", much as English says "attached to", "afraid of": བདག་ནི་འཁོར་བའི་བདེ་བ་ལ་ཆགས། "I am attached to the happiness of saṃsāra." Verbs of perception, on the contrary, mark the one who perceives: ཁོང་གིས་གཟུགས་མཐོང་ངོ་། "He sees a form." With དགོས་ "to need" the one who needs takes ལ་ "to" and the thing needed is bare: མེ་ཏོག་ལ་ཆུ་དགོས། "Flowers need water." The subject is left out whenever the context makes it clear, which English cannot do: དགྲ་ལ་སྡང་། "They hate their enemies."

A command uses a special imperative form of the verb, sometimes on its own: སྡོད། "Sit!", more often with the imperative particle, written ཅིག, ཞིག or ཤིག like the indefinite particle: སྡོད་ཅིག་ "Sit!". A prohibition is མ་ "not" + verb + the particle: སུ་ལ་ཡང་མ་བསྟན་ཅིག་ "Don't show it to anyone!".

The wish that ends almost every prayer, English "may ...", is the verb with པར or བར, followed either by གྱུར་ "became" with ཅིག་ "imperative particle" or by ཤོག་ "may it be so": འགྲོ་བ་ཀུན་སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་གོ་འཕང་ཐོབ་པར་གྱུར་ཅིག་ "May all beings attain the level of Buddhahood!". This is the shape of the first of the four immeasurables: སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་བདེ་བ་དང་བདེ་བའི་རྒྱུ་དང་ལྡན་པར་གྱུར་ཅིག་ "May all sentient beings be endowed with happiness and the causes of happiness."

Someone addressed stands at the start of the sentence without any particle, where English uses a comma or "O": དགེ་སློང་དག་ངོ་མཚར་ལགས། "Monks! It is amazing!". And a verb turned into a noun by པ་ "nominalizing particle" keeps its agent and objects, like the English participle in "taught by the Buddha": སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་བསྟན་པ་ནི་ "regarding the teachings given by the Buddha, ...".

## lesson-05

Seven particles, written ལ, སུ, ཏུ, དུ, ར, རུ and ན, are known in Tibetan grammar by the one name la-dön, "the meanings of ལ". They are one particle in seven spellings, and together they do the work of several English prepositions, "in", "at", "to", "for", and of the English indirect object.

The spelling depends on the last letter of the syllable before the particle: after ས write སུ; after ག and བ write ཏུ; after ང, ད, ན, མ, ར and ལ write དུ; after an open syllable a fused ར: དགོན་པར་མཁས་པ་དག་གིས་བསྟན་བཅོས་འཆད་དོ། "The scholars explain the treatises in the monastery." After a final འ the fused ར replaces that letter: ལྷ་ཚོགས་ནམ་མཁར་སྣང་ངོ་། "The host of deities appears in the sky." In verse the separate syllable རུ is used. Two particles stand apart: ལ is written after any letter, and ན only ever marks a location.

The main meaning is location in place and time: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་རྒྱ་གར་དུ་ཆོས་བསྟན་ནོ། "The Buddha taught the Dharma in India.", ཁོང་དགོན་པ་ན་བཞུགས། "He stays at the monastery." Words of place such as ནང་ "inside" and རྒྱབ་ "back, behind" are joined to the noun by the connective particle and themselves take a la-dön particle, the mirror image of English "inside of": དགོན་པའི་ནང་དུ་ "inside the monastery", ཁང་པའི་རྒྱབ་ཏུ་ "behind the house".

The second meaning is the recipient, the English indirect object: སྨན་པས་ནད་པ་ལ་སྨན་སྟེར། "The doctor gives medicine to the sick person." Some transitive verbs mark their object with ལ་ "to", as English says "look at": བདག་གིས་ཁོང་ལ་ལྟ། "I look at him." The noun དོན་ "benefit, purpose" with a la-dön particle, after a connective particle, means "for the sake of": དུས་གསུམ་གྱི་དགེ་བ་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་དོན་དུ་སྔོའོ། "One dedicates the virtue of the three times for the benefit of all sentient beings."

An adjective with a la-dön particle becomes an adverb: བླ་མས་བསྟན་བཅོས་གསལ་པོར་འཆད་དོ། "The guru explains the treatises clearly." Some such adverbs mirror Sanskrit prefixes, ཤིན་ཏུ་ "very, extremely" for ati-: ཤིན་ཏུ་ལྷག་པ་ "very abundant". The particle can mean "as", setting one thing equal to another: སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཕ་མར་ཤེས། "One knows all sentient beings to be one's parents." Between a noun and a verb it forms a compound verb: ཉམས་སུ་ལེན་པ་ "to practice", literally "to take into experience". After a verb, པར or བར joins it to a verb of knowing, English "that": སེམས་ཀྱི་ངོ་བོ་སྟོང་པ་ཉིད་ཡིན་པར་རྟོགས། "One understands that the essence of mind is emptiness." ལ་ "to" after a word can, like ནི་ "as for", mark the topic: དེ་ལ་ "as for that, ...". And ན་ "if, when" after a verb means "if" or "when": དེ་ལ་དབྱེ་ན་ "if one divides that, ...".

Two more particles belong to this lesson. ཀྱང་ "also, even", written ཡང after ང, ན, མ, ར and ལ and as a fused འང after an open syllable, means "also" or "even": གཅིག་ཀྱང་མེད། "There does not exist even one." The particle "or" repeats the final letter of the word and adds མ: རྟག་པའམ་མི་རྟག་པ་ "permanent or impermanent", དྲུག་གམ་བདུན་ "six or seven". Placed after the last verb, the same particle turns the sentence into a question, which English does by changing the word order: ཁོང་བོད་པ་ཡིན་ནམ། "Is he Tibetan?"

## lesson-06

Two particles, ནས་ "from, since" and ལས་ "from", answer the question "from where": the source, the starting point, the origin: སྤྲིན་ལས་ཆར་འབྱུང་ངོ་། "Rain comes forth from the clouds." ནས་ "from" paired with བར་ "until" and a la-dön particle gives "from ... to": བོད་ནས་བལ་ཡུལ་གྱི་བར་དུ་ "from Tibet to Nepal".

ལས་ "from" also opens a quotation by naming the text, and ཞེས་ "closing quotation particle", written ཅེས after ག, ད and བ, closes it, doing the work of English quotation marks: གཞུང་འདི་ལས་ཆོས་ཐམས་ཅད་མི་རྟག་པ་ཡིན་ཞེས་གསུངས་སོ། "It is said in this text: 'all phenomena are impermanent'." When a person is quoted, the person takes the agentive particle: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་ཆོས་ཀུན་བདག་མེད་པ་ཡིན་ཞེས་གསུངས་སོ། "The Buddha said: 'all phenomena are selfless'."

Comparison is built with ལས་ "from": the thing compared with, then the particle, then the adjective. English says "bigger than that house"; Tibetan says, as it were, "from that house, big": དགོན་པ་འདི་ཁང་པ་དེ་ལས་ཆེ་བའོ། "This monastery is bigger than that house." In the same place one also finds པས or བས: དགོན་པ་འདི་ཁང་པ་དེ་བས་ཆེ་བའོ། "This monastery is bigger than that house." With a negated verb ལས་ "from" means "except", "other than": རྩ་བའི་བླ་མ་ལས་རེ་ས་མེད་དོ། "There is no place of hope apart from the root guru." Both particles can single out one member of a group, "among": ངན་སོང་གསུམ་ནས་ "amongst the three lower realms, ...". They form adverbs: གདོད་ནས་ "primordially, from the beginning". And ལས་ "from" can mark the means or the reason: བདག་གིས་ཐབས་འདི་ལས་ཐར་པ་ཐོབ་བོ། "I attained liberation through this method."

The second theme is how a clause is attached to a noun, the English relative clause with "who" or "which". Tibetan nominalises the verb with པ་ "nominalizing particle", written བ after ང, ར, ལ and an open syllable, adds the connective particle and puts the whole clause before the noun, as English does with a participle in "the Dharma-teaching Buddha": ཆོས་བསྟན་པའི་སངས་རྒྱས། "the Buddha who taught the Dharma". The clause can also follow the noun, with no particle between: སངས་རྒྱས་ཆོས་བསྟན་པ། "the Buddha who taught the Dharma". The noun need not be the agent of the clause; it may be its object: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་བསྟན་པའི་ཆོས་ "the Dharma that the Buddha taught".

The noun and its clause form one unit, and the particle after that unit shows its role in the main sentence: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་སྡིག་པ་བྱེད་པའི་མི་ལ་ཆོས་བསྟན་ནོ། "The Buddha taught the Dharma to the man who commits evil deeds."

Question words such as སུ་ "who" and གང་ "what, which" do not move to the front as in English; they usually stand right before the verb: རྒྱུ་མཚན་གང་ཡིན། "What is the reason?", བླ་མ་འདི་སུ་ཡིན། "Who is this guru?" With ཡང་ "also, even" and a negated verb they give "no one", "nothing"; the negation stays on the verb, where English puts it on the pronoun: རི་ཁྲོད་དུ་སུ་ཡང་མེད་དོ། "There is no one in the mountain hermitage."

## lesson-07

The last lesson is about how Tibetan marks time and how it chains clauses into the long sentences that can run through half a page of a text.

Tense is carried by auxiliary verbs after the main verb. On its own འགྱུར་ "to become" means "to become", and what one becomes takes a la-dön particle: ལྕགས་གསེར་དུ་འགྱུར། "Iron changes into gold." Its past form གྱུར་ "became" often translates simply as "to be": དགྲར་གྱུར་པའི་ཉོན་མོངས་ "afflictive emotions that are the enemies". As an auxiliary, after the main verb with པར or བར, it gives the future, English "will": འགྲོ་བ་ཀུན་ནི་འཆི་བར་འགྱུར། "All beings will die." The negation goes right before the auxiliary. The second helper is བྱེད་པ་ "to do" with three forms, བྱས་ "did" for the past, བྱེད་ "to do" for the present, བྱ་ "will do, should" for the future; it marks the tense and stresses the agent: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་ཆོས་བསྟན་པར་བྱས། "The Buddha taught the Dharma." The future form usually means "must", "should": ཆོས་བསྟན་པར་བྱ། "One should teach the Dharma." After an intransitive verb the same helper makes it causative: ཐར་པར་བྱེད་པ་ "to liberate (to cause to be liberated)". A verb followed by བྱ་ "object of the action" names what is to be done to: ཤེས་བྱ་ "object of knowledge". Other auxiliaries add their own meaning: ནུས་ "to be able", དགོས་ "to need", འདོད་ "to want".

Clauses are linked by particles after the verb, many of them familiar ones in a new role. The connective particle after a verb means "but", "although": ཚིག་ཤེས་ཀྱི་དོན་མི་གོའོ། "One knows the words but does not understand the meaning." ཀྱང་ "also, even" after a verb is "although", "even though": བདག་ལ་ཡོན་ཏན་མེད་ཀྱང་དགེ་བའི་བཤེས་གཉེན་ལ་དད། "I have no qualities but I have faith in the spiritual guide." The agentive particle after a verb means "because", "therefore": ཁོང་ནི་ལེ་ལོ་ཡིན་པས་བླ་མའི་གདམས་ངག་ལ་མི་ཉན། "Since he is lazy, he doesn't listen to the oral instructions of the guru." Sometimes it is temporal, "as", "when": སངས་རྒྱས་འཇིག་རྟེན་དུ་བྱོན་པས་ཆོས་བསྟན། "As the Buddha came into the world, he taught the Dharma." A verb with པའི or བའི followed by ཕྱིར་ "for the sake of; because" gives a reason or a purpose; followed by དུ it always marks a purpose, followed by ན always a reason: རྣམ་གཡེང་སྤང་བའི་ཕྱིར་དུ་དབེན་པར་གནས་པར་བྱའོ། "In order to abandon distractions, one must stay in a solitary place."

Sequence of actions. དང་ "and" after a verb links two actions, "and", "as", "upon": རྩ་བའི་བླ་མ་དྲན་པ་དང་མོས་གུས་སྐྱེས་སོ། "As one remembered the root guru, devotion was born." ཞིང་ "and", written ཅིང after ག, ད and བ and ཤིང after ས, joins two verbs with the same subject, like an English "-ing" form: ཁོང་གིས་བླ་མ་ལ་མཆོད་པ་འབུལ་ཞིང་ཕྱག་འཚལ་ལོ། "He makes offerings to the guru and prostrates to him." A verb with ནས་ "after, having done" means "after having ...": ཕ་མའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་དྲན་ནས་སྙིང་རྗེ་སྐྱེས་སོ། "Remembering the suffering of one's parents, compassion was born." A verb with ལ་ "to" simply joins two clauses: བལ་ཡུལ་དུ་སོང་ལ་བྱང་སེམས་སྐྱེད་ཅིག་ "Go to Nepal and generate bodhicitta!" A negated verb with པར or བར means "without ...ing": དགེ་བ་མེད་པར་བདེ་བ་མེད། "Without virtue, there is no happiness." A verb with པ་ "nominalizing particle" and ལ་ "to" expresses purpose, "in order to": སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་གོ་འཕང་ཐོབ་པ་ལ་ཚོགས་གཉིས་གསོག་ "To attain the level of Buddhahood, one gathers the two accumulations." One sentence can hold ལ་ "to" in three roles, place, object and purpose: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱིས་རྒྱ་གར་ལ་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ལ་ཕན་པ་ལ་ཆོས་བསྟན་ནོ། "The Buddha taught the Dharma in India to benefit all sentient beings." A verb with ན་ "if, when" is "if" or "when", and གལ་ཏེ་ "if" at the start makes "if" unambiguous: གལ་ཏེ་མེ་ཡོད་ན་དུ་བ་འབྱུང་བར་འགྱུར་རོ། "If there is fire, smoke will appear."

The continuative particle stands apart. It is written ཏེ after ན, ར, ལ and ས, སྟེ after ག, ང, བ, མ and an open syllable, དེ after ད. It links clauses in sequence: གྲྭ་པས་བསྒོམས་ཏེ་སྦྱིན་བདག་དགོན་པར་ཕྱིན་ནོ། "While the monk was meditating, the benefactor went to the monastery." It introduces a list or a definition, like a colon: སྔོན་འགྲོ་ལ་གཉིས་ཏེ་ཐུན་མོང་དང་ཐུན་མོང་མ་ཡིན་པའི་སྔོན་འགྲོའོ། "Regarding the preliminaries, there are two: the common and the extraordinary preliminaries." And it builds the syllogism of the philosophical texts, statement + continuative particle, reason + ཕྱིར་ "because": མེ་ཡོད་དེ་དུ་བ་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ། "There is fire because there is smoke."

## bialek-01

This first lesson is about the noun phrase: a noun and the words that describe it. In Classical Tibetan the noun comes first and everything that qualifies it follows, so the English order is reversed. A word has one fixed form; there is no article, no plural ending and no agreement between a noun and its adjective.

The adjective follows its noun: ཆོས་ཟབ་མོ་ "profound Buddhist teaching", ཚིག་འཇམ་པ་ "mild words", ཁྲག་དྲོན་མོ་ "warm blood". English "profound teaching" becomes, word for word, "teaching profound". Nothing marks the plural here: བུ་མོ་ངན་པ་ "bad daughters" is the same phrase as "bad daughter", and the context decides.

Numerals also follow the noun: ལོ་གསུམ་ "three years", ཞག་དུ་མ་ "many days". Here too English puts the number first. The demonstrative དེ་ "that" stands after the whole phrase: རྒྱལ་པོ་དེ་ "that king", སྲས་ཐ་ཆུངས་དེ་ "the youngest son". Tibetan has no definite article, and this demonstrative is often best rendered by the English "the".

Where English says "a" or "some", Tibetan puts the indefinite particle ཞིག་ "a, an" at the end of the phrase: བྲག་རོག་པོ་ཞིག་ "a black rock". Its spelling changes with the last letter of the syllable before it; ཞིག is the form after a vowel and after ང, ན, མ, འ, ར and ལ, and the other forms will come in later lessons.

Putting it together, the order is noun, then numeral, then the indefinite particle, which always closes the phrase: ཕུག་རོན་གསུམ་ཞིག་ "three doves", literally "doves three some". Reading from the noun outward, and remembering that the last word of a phrase is a particle rather than its head, is the habit to form in these first exercises.

## bialek-02

A title and a name stand side by side, the title first and the name after it: རྒྱལ་བུ་སེམས་ཅན་ཆེན་པོ་དེ་ "the prince Semchen Chenpo". This is apposition: two nouns for one person, with no particle between them. A demonstrative or any other qualifier then follows the whole pair.

Tibetan does not mark number on the noun itself. A numeral after the noun does it: སེམས་ཅན་ཁྲི་ "ten thousand sentient beings", སྤྲེའུ་ཕྲུག་ལྔ་བརྒྱ་ "five hundred monkey-children". So does a word of quantity such as མང་པོ་ "many" or the adverbial form མང་དུ་ "many, much": བརྡ་མང་དུ་ "many signs". When plurality matters and nothing else shows it, the plural particle རྣམས་ "plural particle" or དག་ "plural particle" is added at the very end: བུ་མོ་དེ་དག་ "the daughters". The phrase འཁོར་མང་པོ་རྣམས་ "many attendants" shows the adjective after the noun and the plural particle closing the phrase.

The word ཀུན་ "all" also comes last in the noun phrase: ཁྱིམ་མཚེས་དྲག་པ་ཀུན་ "all the noble neighbours". So the full order is noun, adjective, numeral, demonstrative, and then ཀུན་ "all" or a plural particle; most phrases use only a part of it: རྒྱལ་པོ་ཆེན་པོ་ "the great king", སྟོན་མོ་བཟང་པོ་ "an excellent feast".

In the sentence the verb comes last. The copula ཡིན་ "is, am, are" closes the sentence, and the subject before it takes no case particle at all, nor does the noun it is equated with: བདག་རྒྱལ་བུ་སེམས་ཅན་ཆེན་པོ་ཡིན། "I am the prince Semchen Chenpo.", ང་དགེ་བསྙེན་ཡིན། "I am a lay practitioner."

English puts the copula between the two nouns and changes it with the person, "I am", "he is"; Tibetan has the one form ཡིན་ "is, am, are" for every person and puts it at the end. The two pronouns ང་ "I" and བདག་ "I" mean the same, the second being the more literary. The vertical stroke after the verb closes the sentence like an English full stop.

## bialek-03

The genitive particle links two nouns so that the first belongs to or describes the second. It corresponds to the English "of" or the possessive "'s", but the order is that of "'s": the possessor comes first, then the particle, then the thing possessed: སྤྲེའུའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་ཆེན་པོ་ "the great king of monkeys", བུད་མེད་ཀྱི་ཆས་ "a woman's garments". A phrase is read from the end backwards.

The spelling depends on the last letter of the syllable before the particle. After ག and ང write གི; after ད, བ and ས write ཀྱི; after ན, མ, ར and ལ write གྱི; after an open syllable, and after a final འ, write འི fused to that syllable: བདག་ཅག་གི་ཚེ་སྲོག་ "our lives", ཁ་བ་ཅན་གྱི་རྒྱལ་ཁམས་འདི་ "this realm of the Snowy Land (Tibet)", ངའི་སྡོམ་པ་ "my vow". Possessive pronouns are simply a pronoun plus the genitive. In verse, where the count of syllables matters, the fused འི is replaced by the separate syllable ཡི་ "of": རྒྱ་མཚོ་ཆེན་པོ་ཆུ་ཡི་གཏེར་ཡིན། "A vast ocean is a treasury of water."

The verb ཡོད་ "to exist, there is; to have" states that something exists or is somewhere, English "there is". The place takes the locative particle ན་ "in, at": དེའི་ཚེ་ཡུལ་དེ་ན་བྲམ་ཟེ་ཕྱིན་ཏེ་ལོ་ཤུ་ཤ་ཡོད་དོ། "At that time in that country there was a Brahmin called Phyinteloshusha."

English "to have" is expressed with the same verb. The possessor takes the dative particle ལ་ "to", the thing possessed has no particle, and the verb closes the sentence, literally "to the uncle there are many sons": ཨ་ཁུ་ལ་བུ་མང་པོ་ཡོད། "The paternal uncle had many sons." Location and possession can share one sentence: གྲོང་ཁྱེར་དེ་ན་རྒན་མོ་ཞིག་ལ་བུ་གཉིས་ཤིག་ཡོད། "In the town an old woman had two sons." Here the indefinite particle appears in two spellings, ཞིག after a vowel and ཤིག after ས.

A sentence in a classical text is often closed by a final particle. After a vowel it is the fused འོ; after a consonant it repeats that consonant with the vowel o, so after ད it is written དོ and after ན it is ནོ: ཁྱོད་ཀྱི་སྐད་སྙན་པ་ཡིན་ནོ། "Your voice is pleasant." It adds nothing to the meaning; it marks the end of a statement, as the full stop does.

## bialek-04

A Tibetan sentence ends with its verb, and the verb carries no ending for person or number. Intransitive verbs, verbs whose subject does not act on an object, such as "rise", "be born", "go", take their subject with no case particle at all, bare, exactly like an English subject: རུས་བུ་ཚལ་ན་འདུག་གོ། "Bones lie in the wood.", སྤྲེའུ་ཕྲུག་རྣམས་སྐྱེས་སོ། "Monkey-children were born.", རྒྱལ་བུ་གཉིས་འོངས་སོ། "Two princes came."

Tense is not marked by an ending either. Most verbs have a separate past stem, and the stems met here are past ones: ལངས་ "rose", འདུས་ "gathered", སོང་ "went". The location takes ན་ "in, at", as in the first example. The final particle repeats the last letter of the verb: after ང it is ངོ, after ས it is སོ, after ག it is གོ. The adverb དེ་ནས་ "thereafter, then", literally "from that", opens many sentences of a story: དེ་ནས་བྲག་སྲིན་མོ་ལངས་སོ། "Thereafter the rock-ogress rose."

The terminative particle marks the goal or result of a movement or a change, English "into", "to", "as". Its spelling depends on the last letter before it: after ག and བ write ཏུ; after ང, ད, ན, མ, ར and ལ write དུ; after ས write སུ; after a vowel a fused ར: བདག་སྲིན་མོའི་རིགས་སུ་སྐྱེས་སོ། "I was born into the race of ogresses.", ལྷ་ཕྱིར་གནས་སུ་སོང་ངོ་། "The deity went back to its place."

With the verb གྱུར་ "became" the terminative names what something turns into, the pattern X + ཏུ + གྱུར་ "became X": གོས་ངུར་སྨྲིག་ཏུ་གྱུར་ཏོ། "The clothes turned saffron." The final particle here is ཏོ་ "terminating particle", the form this verb takes because of an old second final ད.

The demonstrative དེ་ "that" with the fused ར gives the adverb "there": རྒྱལ་བུ་སེམས་ཅན་ཆེན་པོ་དེ་དེར་ཚེ་འཕོས་སོ། "The prince Semchen Chenpo passed away there.", དེ་དག་དེར་སོང་ངོ་། "They went there."

The recipient or possessor takes ལ་ "to", as in the last lesson: ཡབ་མེས་རྣམས་ལ་ནོར་བྱུང་ངོ་། "Wealth came to the ancestors." With མེད་ "there is not", the negative of ཡོད་ "there is", the same pattern says "has no": མི་དེ་ལ་བུ་ཕོ་མེད། "This man has no sons."

## bialek-05

Transitive verbs, verbs with an object such as "kill", "see", "think", treat their subject differently from English. English leaves both subject and object unmarked and tells them apart by order. Tibetan marks the agent, the one who acts, with the ergative particle, and leaves the object bare. The order is agent, object, verb: ཁྱོད་ཀྱིས་ངའི་ཁྱོ་བསད་དོ། "You killed my husband.", བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་ཆོས་བསྟན་ཏོ། "The Victorious One taught the Dharma."

The ergative has the forms of the genitive with ས added. After ག and ང write གིས; after ད, བ and ས write ཀྱིས; after ན, མ, ར and ལ write གྱིས; after a vowel the particle is a bare ས fused to the word, so དེ་ "that, he" becomes དེས, and ཆུང་མ་ "wife" takes the same bare ས: དེ་ནས་རྒྱལ་བུ་ཐ་ཆུངས་དེས་ཡིད་ལ་འདི་སྙམ་དུ་བསམས་སོ། "Then the youngest prince thought this in his mind.", ཆུང་མས་རྟག་ཏུ་སྤྱོའོ། "The wife is always scolding." Since the particle attaches to the last word, it marks the whole phrase before it, "the youngest prince" as one unit.

The same particle marks the instrument, English "with" or "by means of", and a sentence may then contain two of them, one for the agent and one for the tool: དེའི་ཚེ་རྒྱལ་བུས་ཤིང་གི་ཚལ་བ་རྣོན་པོས་ལུས་ལ་ཁྲག་ཕྱུང་ངོ་། "Then, with a sharp wooden splinter, the prince let blood out of his body.", བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་དེའི་བསམ་པ་ཐུགས་ཀྱིས་མཁྱེན་ནོ། "The Victorious One discerned his thoughts with his mind." It can also give the cause, "because of": བུ་ཚས་འཁོར་བའི་འདམ་དུ་བདག་ཚུད་དོ། "Because of my offspring I got into the swamp of samsara."

Two nouns are joined by དང་ "and; with", placed between them like the English "and": སྐྲ་དང་ཁ་སྤུ་རང་བྱིའོ། "The hair and beard fell off by themselves." A pair often closes with the numeral གཉིས་ "two", which English leaves out: ཨ་ནེ་དང་ཨ་ཁུ་གཉིས་གཅིག་ཏུ་དྲིལ་ལོ། "The aunt and the uncle united."

Intransitive verbs keep their subject bare, as before: རྐང་པ་ཆག་གོ། "The leg broke.", གླང་སྒོ་གཞན་དུ་སོང་ངོ་། "The ox went out through another door." Whether a verb is transitive or not must therefore be learnt with the verb, since the particle on the subject depends on it.

## bialek-06

Two particles answer "from where". The elative ནས་ "from, since" marks the point from which something starts, in space or in time: བདག་ཡུན་རིང་པོ་ནས་འཁོར་བ་ན་འཁོར་རོ། "For a long time I have been roaming in samsara." The delative ལས་ "from" marks the person or thing from which something is taken or received: དེས་ཁྱིམ་བདག་ཅིག་ལས་གླང་ཞིག་བརྙས་སོ། "He borrowed an ox from a householder." Both answer to English "from"; the elative is "out of", "since", the delative "away from".

Direction towards is the terminative. With ཆུ་ "water" the fused ར gives "into the water": སྟེའུ་ཆུར་ལྷུང་ངོ་། "The axe fell into the river." Before the verb འགྱུར་ "to become" a noun or adjective in the terminative names what something turns into, X + ར + འགྱུར་ "becomes X": འབྱུང་པོའི་བྱ་རྣམས་ལོང་བར་འགྱུར་རོ། "The birds of demons become blind." The past of the same idea uses སོང་ "went": ཡན་ལག་རྣམས་སེར་གར་སོང་ངོ་། "The limbs became cracked."

Negation stands immediately before the verb, where English needs "not" after an auxiliary. The present and future take མི་ "not": བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་བསམ་གྱིས་མི་ཁྱབ་བོ། "The Victorious One cannot be grasped by thought." Here the ergative on བསམ་ "to think" marks the instrument, "by thought". The adjective སྡུག་ "pretty, nice" negated in the same way gives "ugly": བྲམ་ཟེ་དེའི་ཆུང་མ་དེ་མི་སྡུག་གོ། "The wife of that Brahmin was ugly."

A command uses the imperative stem of the verb, alone or followed by the imperative particle, written ཅིག after ག, ད and བ, ཞིག after ང, ན, མ, འ, ར, ལ and a vowel, ཤིག after ས: གླང་སླར་བྱིན་ཅིག་ "Give the ox back!", ད་ཟོ། "Now eat!" The subject "you" is not expressed, as in English.

The patterns already known continue. The agent of a transitive verb takes the ergative: ཨ་མས་གྲལ་གྱི་དབུས་སུ་ལངས་སོ། "The mother stood up in the middle of the row.", ཞང་པོས་ཡབ་ཀྱི་ཞལ་ཆེམས་ཀྱི་ཡི་གེ་བཀླགས་སོ། "The maternal uncle read the text of the father's testament." Existence in a place uses ཡོད་ "there is" with ན་ "in, at": ལམ་གྱི་བར་དེ་ན་ཆུ་བོ་ཞིག་ཡོད། "In the middle of the road there was a river."

## bialek-07

Nominal particles are small syllables added after a word or a phrase to make a noun or an adjective of it. The most important is པ་ "nominalizing particle", written བ after ང, འ, ར, ལ and a vowel and པ after the other final letters. Attached to a verb it gives what English makes with "-ing" or a participle: རྐུ་ "to steal" with the particle means "stealing", "thieving", and placed after a noun this participle qualifies it like an adjective: དེའི་ཚེ་ན་གྲོང་ཁྱེར་དེ་ན་རྒན་མོ་ཞིག་ལ་བུ་རྐུ་བ་གཉིས་ཤིག་ཡོད། "At that time in the town an old woman had two thieving sons."

A whole verb phrase can be nominalised in this way, negation included, and then used as a noun, English "the one who ...": མི་མཐོང་བ་དེ་ལ་བུ་ཕོ་མེད། "The blind one had no son.", literally "to the not-seeing one there is no son". A clause with its own subject becomes in the same way the object of a verb of perceiving: ལྷ་མོ་དེས་དེ་སྐད་སྨྲས་པ་ཐོས་སོ། "The queen heard these words uttered." Here ལྷ་མོ་ "goddess" is the word used for the queen.

Before a verb of becoming the nominalised adjective takes the terminative: ཚིམ་པ་ "satisfied" with ར and འགྱུར་ "to become" gives "becomes satisfied": ཤ་རློན་པ་དང་།ཁྲག་དྲོན་མོས་དེའི་ཡིད་ཚིམ་པར་འགྱུར་རོ། "Her mind is satisfied with fresh flesh and warm blood." The ergative on the second noun marks the means, "with flesh and blood", and the stroke after དང་ "and" inside the sentence is a pause, not the end of a sentence.

The ergative also marks a cause. A phrase like "by the power of karma" ends in it: བདག་ལས་ཀྱི་དབང་གིས་སྲིན་མོའི་རིགས་སུ་སྐྱེས་སོ། "By the power of my past actions I was born into the race of ogresses." The word དབང་ "power" is joined to ལས་ "action, karma" by the genitive, and the whole phrase takes the ergative.

The ordinary agent takes it too: ཁྱོད་ཀྱིས་ངའི་གླང་བོར་རོ། "You lost my ox.", འདི་དག་གིས་ང་ཁྲིད་དོ། "They are leading me." And a command with the imperative particle: ཤིང་མཁན་གྱི་མདུན་སོ་གཉིས་ཆོག་ཅིག་ "Knock out the carpenter's two front teeth!"

## bialek-08

So far every sentence had one verb. Tibetan joins clauses into longer sentences by putting a particle after the verb of each clause that is not the last; only the final verb takes the final particle. Such a verb with its particle is a converb. The clauses follow the order of the events, so a long sentence is read clause by clause from the beginning.

The coordinative particle, written ཅིང after ག, ད and བ, ཞིང after ང, ན, མ, འ, ར, ལ and a vowel, and ཤིང after ས, joins two verbs that happen together or one after the other, usually with the same subject. English uses "and" or an "-ing" form: སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་བསྟན་པ་དར་ཞིང་རྒྱས་སོ། "The teaching of the Buddha spread and increased.", ཕུག་རོན་གསུམ་ཞིག་ཀུན་ཏུ་འཕུར་ཞིང་རྩེའོ། "Three doves were flying around and playing."

A prohibition is built like a command, with མ་ "not" before the imperative stem and the imperative particle after it: གཞན་དུ་མ་སེམས་ཤིག་ "Don't think otherwise!" A plain command for comparison: ཁོ་བོའི་ཕྲིན་ཞིག་རྒྱལ་པོ་ལ་བྱོས་ཤིག་ "Deliver my message to the king!"

Classical Tibetan has a register of respect: for the actions and belongings of honoured persons it uses special words that replace the ordinary ones. བཞུགས་ "to stay, abide (h.)" is the honorific of འདུག་ "to be there, to sit, to stay": དུས་གཅིག་ན།བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་མཉན་དུ་ཡོད་པ་ན་བཞུགས་སོ། "One day the Victorious One was abiding in Shravasti." The stroke after the opening phrase marks a pause. Some verbs are made of a noun and a verb; "rejoiced" is literally "the mind delighted": དེའི་ཚེ་ན་ཀུན་དགའ་བོ་དང་།འཁོར་མང་པོ་ཡི་རངས་སོ། "Then Ananda and the numerous attendants rejoiced."

"To take X as Y" puts X with ལ་ "to", Y in the terminative, and the verb འཛིན་ "to hold, grasp, take as": བདག་ད་ལྟར་ཁྱིམ་ལ་དུར་ཙམ་དུ་འཛིན་ནོ། "Now I take my house merely as a grave." The word ཙམ་ "mere, merely" after a noun restricts it, "merely a grave". The terminative with གྱུར་ "became" names what something becomes: མཁའ་ལྡིང་གོས་སེར་ཅན་གྱི་བཞོན་པར་གྱུར་ཏོ། "The garuḍa became the mount of Vishnu, the Yellow-clad."

## bialek-09

The focus particle ནི་ "as for" marks the topic of the sentence, the thing the sentence is about, and it follows that phrase. English has nothing so light; "as for ..." is the nearest, and often the particle is simply left untranslated: རྒྱལ་བུ་གཉིས་ནི་འོངས་སོ། "As for the two princes, they came.", བདག་ནི་རྒྱལ་བུ་སེམས་ཅན་ཆེན་པོ་ཡིན། "As for myself, I am the prince Semchen Chenpo." In the stories that identify the persons of a past life with those of the present, the particle sets off the past person before the copula names the present one: དེ་ནི་ད་ལྟར་ངའི་ཡབ་རྒྱལ་པོ་ཟས་གཙང་མ་ཡིན་ནོ། "As for him, now he is my father, the king Shuddhodana."

The gerundial particle is a converbal particle: it closes a clause whose verb is not the last. It is written ཏེ after ན, ར, ལ and ས, སྟེ after ག, ང, བ, མ, འ and a vowel, and དེ after ད. Its usual sense is "having done X, then Y", English "and" or a participle: དེས་རྡོ་ཞིག་བླངས་ཏེ་འཕངས་སོ། "He took a stone and threw it." It can also introduce what the next clause explains, like a colon or a semicolon: ཕུག་རོན་བུ་ཕོའི་བླ་སྟེ།ཕུག་རོན་ནང་གི་ཆུང་ངུ་ཁྲས་ཁྱེར་རོ། "A dove is a sign for a son; the youngest among the doves was carried away by a falcon." The stroke after the particle marks the pause between the clauses.

The diminutive particle makes "the little one" of a word: ཆུང་ངུ་ "the youngest; childhood" is "the small one", and among several it means the youngest, as in the sentence about the doves.

A name is often followed by བྱ་བ་ "called, so-called", literally "to be done", where English says "called": དེར་སྤྲེའུ་ཕྲུག་དྲུག་པོ་དེ་ནི་ཕ་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སྤྲེའུས།ནགས་མ་བྱ་ཚོགས་བྱ་བ།ཤིང་ཐོག་མོད་པ་ཞིག་ཏུ་བསྐྱལ་ཏོ། "Then, as for these six monkey-children, their father, the bodhisattva-monkey, brought them to the forest called Flock of Birds, abounding in fruits." The same sentence shows an apposition of title and name, "father, the bodhisattva-monkey", closed by the ergative, and strokes that cut the long sentence into phrases.

A list may open with its generic word and close with the number of members: ཕྱུགས་བ་ར་བོང་གསུམ་ "domesticated cattle: cows, goats and donkeys, the three".

## bialek-10

The concessive particle, written ཀྱང after ག, ད, བ and ས, ཡང after ང, ན, མ, ར and ལ, and འང fused after a vowel or འ, follows a noun phrase or a verb. After a noun it means "also", "too", "even", and it comes after any case particle: རྒྱལ་པོས་ཀྱང་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱི་བཀའ་བཞིན་དུ་དེ་དག་བཏང་ངོ་། "Indeed, the king set them free according to the words of the Victorious One." Repeated in two parallel clauses it gives "X as well as Y": སྤུ་ཡང་ཐུང་དུ་སོང་།མཇུག་མ་ཡང་ཐུང་དུ་སོང་ངོ་། "Their hair grew shorter; their tails grew shorter too." In this last sentence ཐུང་ "to be short" with the terminative and སོང་ "went" expresses a change of state, "grew shorter".

After a verb the same particle means "even if", "although", and the clause it closes is not the last one: བལ་པོས་སེ་འུ་མ་ཟོས་ཀྱང་།ཁ་དོག་ཉིད་ལས་བྲོ་བ་ཤེས། "Even if a Nepalese did not eat the pomegranate, he knows its taste from its very colour." Here the past is negated with མ་ "not", and ལས་ "from" gives the source of the knowledge.

Where English has prepositions before the noun, Tibetan has postpositions after it. A postposition is itself a noun of place or manner: it is joined to the preceding noun by the genitive, or directly, and then takes a case particle of its own. ནང་ "inside" with the terminative gives "into": དབྱིག་པ་ཅན་གྱིས་གླང་དེ་ཁྱིམ་གྱི་ནང་དུ་བཏང་ངོ་། "Yikpachen let the ox into the house." དྲུང་ "presence, vicinity" with the locative gives "under", "near": དེ་བཞིན་གཤེགས་པ་ཤིང་དྲུང་ན་བཞུགས་སོ། "The Tathagata was sitting under a tree." བཞིན་ "manner" with the terminative gives "according to", as in the first example above.

The delative ལས་ "from" also marks descent, with ཆད་པ་ "descended, born (from)": བོད་ཁ་བ་ཅན་པ་མི་འདི་རྣམས་ཕ་སྤྲེའུ་དང་།མ་བྲག་སྲིན་མོ་གཉིས་ལས་ཆད་པ་ཡིན་ནོ། "These people of snowy Tibet are descended from a father-monkey and a mother rock-ogress."

The verb ལྡན་ "to be possessed of, to have" takes what one is endowed with in the terminative, and ནས་ "from, since" marks a starting point in time: སྲས་ཐ་ཆུངས་དེ་ཆུང་ངུ་ནས་བྱམས་པ་དང་སྙིང་རྗེར་ལྡན་ཏེ།ཐམས་ཅད་ལ་བུ་གཅིག་པ་དང་འདྲའོ། "The youngest son, filled with love and compassion from childhood, seemed to all like their only child."

## bialek-11

Questions are formed in two ways, and in neither does the word order change, as it does in English. A yes-no question adds the interrogative particle after the final verb; it is written འམ after a vowel, and after a consonant it repeats that consonant and adds མ, so after ར it is རམ: ཅིའི་སླད་དུ་བདག་འབའ་ཞིག་གི་ཕྱིར་མྱ་ངན་གྱི་རྒྱ་མཚོར་ལྟུང་བ་མི་ཚོར་རམ། "Why then, just because of me, do you not notice your fall into the ocean of misery?" A question with an interrogative word, ཅི་ "what" or སུ་ "who", needs no particle; the question word stays in the place of the answer, usually right before the verb: སྟག་མོ་འདིས་ཟས་སུ་ཅི་ཟ། "What does this tigress eat as food?", དེའི་ཚེ་དེའི་དུས་ན་རྒྱལ་པོ་ཤིང་རྟ་ཆེན་པོ་དེ་སུ་ཡིན། "Who is that king Shingta Chenpo of those days of yore?"

The particle པ་ "nominalizing particle" turns a verb or a whole clause into a noun, which then takes case particles like any noun. English needs "what ..." or "the fact that ...": དེའི་ཚེ་ན་ཀུན་དགའ་བོ་དང་།འཁོར་མང་པོ་ཐམས་ཅད་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་གསུངས་པ་ལ་ཡི་རངས་ཏེ་མངོན་པར་བསྟོད་དོ། "Then Ananda and all the numerous attendants rejoiced at what the Victorious One had said and praised him." A nominalised verb with the genitive, བར་ "interval; until" and the terminative gives "while", "during": ཅུང་ཟད་ཅིག་ངལ་བསོ་བའི་བར་དུ་སྲས་གསུམ་པོ་ཚལ་གྱི་ནང་དུ་དོང་ངོ་། "During a short rest the three sons went into a forest."

English attaches a clause to a noun with "who", "which", "that". Tibetan nominalises the clause and either puts it before the noun with the genitive, or after the noun with no particle between. Before the noun: དེ་ནས་འཕགས་པ་སྤྱན་རས་གཟིགས་དེས།རྫུ་འཕྲུལ་སྟོན་པའི་སྤྲེའུ་ཞིག་ལ།དགེ་བསྙེན་གྱི་སྡོམ་པ་བྱིན་ནོ། "Then the Noble Chenrezig conferred the vow of a lay practitioner upon a monkey that was displaying miracles." After the noun: བདག་ཚེ་འཕོས་པའི་རུས་བུ་ཚལ་ན་འདུག་པ་ལ་ཕ་མས་བསྐོར་ཏོ། "My parents surrounded my bones, the bones of the dead me, lying in the wood." The particle after the whole unit, here ལ་ "to", shows the role of the noun with its clause in the main sentence.

A nominalised clause can also stand alone as a description, closed by the final particle: རབ་ཏུ་དབུལ་ཕོངས་པ།བཟའ་བ་དང་བགོ་བ་མེད་པ་ཞིག་གོ། "He was extremely poor, one with neither food nor clothes."

Direct speech is announced by འདི་སྐད་ "these words, thus" and the quotation particle ཅེས་ "closing quotation particle", and the verb of speaking follows: བྲམ་ཟེས་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ལ་འདི་སྐད་ཅེས་གསོལ་ཏོ། "The Brahmin spoke these words to the Victorious One."

## alphabet/1

The Tibetan script was created in the seventh century on the model of an Indian script. Each of the thirty letters is read as a consonant with the vowel a: ཀ is already ka; another vowel is shown by a small sign above or below the letter, and those signs come later. Books and this course use the headed print style, uchen: most letters have a bar at the top, from which the letter hangs. Writing runs left to right, all letters are one height, there are no capitals.

There are no spaces between words. A dot, the tsheg ་, follows every syllable, and a vertical stroke, the shad །, marks the end of a sentence or of one of its parts. So a line of Tibetan is a chain of syllables on dots: ཁང་པ་ "house" is two syllables. Where a word ends, the reader knows from the words and the sense; that is the skill the course trains.

Next to the Tibetan the course shows the Wylie transcription in Latin letters. It records the spelling, not the sound, as if "knight" were spelt out with its k and gh. The vowel a is not written: ཀ is ka, ཁང is khang; the tsheg becomes a space. It is a check on the letters, not a guide to the sound.

The letters go in rows by the place where they are made, and within a row one pattern repeats. The first row: ཀ ka, ཁ kha, ག ga, ང nga. ཀ is a k without a puff of air, the k of "skin"; ཁ is the same with a strong puff, the k of "kick". English has both sounds but never uses them to tell words apart; in Tibetan the puff is the whole difference between two letters. ག lies between g and k, with a light puff. ང is the ng of "sing"; English has it only at the end of a syllable, Tibetan also at the start: ངན་པ་ "bad".

A Tibetan syllable has a tone: fourteen letters are high, sixteen low, and the cards show it for every letter. English has no tone; here the pitch belongs to the syllable. In the first row ཀ and ཁ are high, ག and ང low, and so in every full row: the first two high, the last two low.

The letters ཀ ཁ ག look alike, and the first rounds show them side by side on purpose. Treat them as three different shapes; soon the eye tells them apart. Example words: ཀུན་ "all", ཁོང་ "he, she", གོ་འཕང་ "level", ངོ་བོ་ "essence". For now it is enough to find the first letter in each of them.

## alphabet/2

The second row repeats the pattern of the first one step closer to the teeth: ཅ ca, ཆ cha, ཇ ja, ཉ nya. It is the same four, plain, with a puff, soft, nasal, but the sounds are now of the ch kind. The tones are shared out the same way: ཅ and ཆ are high, ཇ and ཉ are low.

The letter ཅ is the ch of "catch" with the puff of air held back, and with a high tone: ཅིག་ "a, an", ཅག་ "plural particle". The letter ཆ is the same ch with a strong puff: ཆོས་ "Dharma", ཆུ་ "water". An English ch always carries some breath, so the difference is unfamiliar at first and easier to see than to hear: after ཆ an h is clearly audible.

The letter ཇ lies between j and ch, the j of "jam" said softly, with a low tone: ཇི་ "what, how", ཇོ་བོ་ "lord, master". The letter ཉ is the ny of "canyon", also low: ཉི་མ་ "sun", ཉན་ཐོས་ "śrāvaka". In English ny only appears in the middle of a word; in Tibetan it is an ordinary start of a syllable.

In shape ཅ and ཆ differ by one detail, and ཇ resembles ཛ and ཟ from later rows; the course puts look-alike signs next to each other as answer choices, so look at each letter on its own. The word ཆོས་ "Dharma" will appear in the course more often than most, and it is worth remembering whole already now.

## alphabet/3

The third row is the t family: ཏ ta, ཐ tha, ད da, ན na. These are the sounds of t, d and n, so for an English ear this row is the easiest to hear. The pattern is as before: ཏ without a puff, ཐ with a puff, ད soft, ན nasal; ཏ and ཐ are high, ད and ན low.

The letter ཏ is the t of "stop", without a puff and with a high tone. In the vocabulary of the course it stands mostly in particles: ཏུ་ "la-dön particle" and ཏོ་ "terminating particle". The letter ཐ is the t of "top", with a strong puff: ཐབས་ "method", ཐེག་པ་ "vehicle". As with k, English makes both sounds but treats them as one; Tibetan treats them as two letters. The letter ད lies between d and t, with a light puff and a low tone: དང་ "and", དེ་ "that". The letter ན is an ordinary n: ནག་པོ་ "black", ནི་ "as for".

The difficulty of this row is not in the sounds but in the shapes. The letters ཏ, ད, ན, together with ང from the first row and ར from the seventh, are the most similar group in the whole alphabet. Compare them in pairs on the cards: ཏ and ད, ད and ང, ན and ང; once the difference in each pair is visible, the group stops being confusing.

Two words of this row, དང་ "and" and དེ་ "that", belong to the very first sentences of the course, and they will be in view constantly.

## alphabet/4

The fourth row is the p family: པ pa, ཕ pha, བ ba, མ ma. These are the lip sounds, the p, b and m of English. The pattern is as before, and complete for the last time: པ without a puff, ཕ with a puff, བ soft, མ nasal; པ and ཕ are high, བ and མ low. With this row there are sixteen letters, half the alphabet, and all four full rows.

The letter པ is the p of "spin", without a puff: in the course it appears most often as the particle པ་ "nominalizing particle" after another syllable, in words like ཁང་པ་ "house". The letter ཕ is the p of "pin", with a strong puff: ཕ་ "father", ཕན་ "to benefit". The letter བ lies between b and p, with a light puff and a low tone: བོད་ "Tibet", བུ་ "son". The letter མ is an ordinary m: མེ་ "fire", མི་ "man".

The three letters པ, ཕ and བ are built on one base, and they are the ones most often mixed up. The easiest to remember is བ: it is closed at the bottom by a stroke. The letter ཕ differs from པ by a small hook at the head; find it once on the card, and after that it catches the eye.

The particles པ and བ and the endings པོ, བོ, མོ will turn up at every step of the course: they make a root into a noun or an adjective, as in དམར་པོ་ "red" and ཆེན་པོ་ "big". The first lesson explains them in detail; for now it is enough to recognise the letters.

## alphabet/5

The fifth row: ཙ tsa, ཚ tsha, ཛ dza and ཝ wa. The first three follow the familiar pattern: ཙ without a puff, ཚ with a puff, ཛ soft; this row has no nasal, and the fourth place is taken by ཝ, a letter on its own. Tones: ཙ and ཚ are high, ཛ and ཝ are low.

The letter ཙ is the ts of "cats", with a high tone, but at the start of a syllable, where English never puts it: ཙམ་ "mere, merely". The letter ཚ is ts with a strong puff: ཚ་པོ་ "hot", ཚོ་ "plural particle". The letter ཛ lies between dz and ts, as in "adze", and is low; in the vocabulary of the course it stands in རྫོགས་པ་ "perfection", under a superscribed ར, which comes later.

The shapes of these three are easy to remember: ཙ, ཚ and ཛ are ཅ, ཆ and ཇ of the second row with a small stroke added at the top right. So there are no three new shapes to learn here; the eye only has to notice the stroke: with it, ts; without it, ch.

The letter ཝ is the w of "water", a sound English has and uses freely, with a low tone. It is the rarest letter of the alphabet: on its own it appears mostly in borrowed words, and more often it comes in a small form under another letter; the part on subjoined letters tells about that.

## alphabet/6

The sixth row: ཞ zha, ཟ za, འ ’a and ཡ ya. Here the pattern of the row no longer applies: all four letters are low, and each has a character of its own.

The letter ཞ is the zh of "measure", with a low tone; in the Lhasa pronunciation it often sounds almost like sh: ཞིག་ "a, an", ཞེ་སྡང་ "anger". English has this sound only inside or at the end of a word, never at the start except in borrowings like "genre"; in Tibetan it begins syllables. The letter ཟ is the z of "zoo", also low, and it just as often drifts toward s: ཟབ་མོ་ "profound", ཟ་ཁང་ "restaurant". The English zh and z are always fully voiced; the Tibetan ཞ and ཟ are softer and less voiced, and their surest mark is the low tone. In shape ཞ and ཟ resemble each other and ཝ; look at them closely.

The letter འ, named a-chung, "small a", is special. It has no consonant sound of its own: it holds a vowel with a low tone. In Wylie it is written as an apostrophe, so འོད་དཔག་མེད་ "Amitābha" begins with ’od. The letter has other roles too: it can be a silent prefix before a root, and as འི་ "of" it fuses with an open syllable and becomes the connective particle. All this comes in the part on prefixes and in the first lessons.

The letter ཡ is the y of "yes", with a low tone: ཡིན་ "is", ཡོན་ཏན་ "quality". Like ཝ, it can be written in a small form under another letter, and there it changes the sound much more noticeably; the part on subjoined letters tells about that.

## alphabet/7

The seventh row: ར ra, ལ la, ཤ sha, ས sa. These are the four busiest letters of the alphabet: besides its own sound, each has a second and a third life. ར and ལ are written under other letters, ར, ལ and ས are written above them, and ར, ལ and ས also serve as suffixes of a syllable. That is why they will appear in almost every sentence. Tones: ར and ལ are low, ཤ and ས are high.

The letter ར is an r, but not the English one, which is made without the tongue touching anything: here the tongue gives a single light tap, lightly rolled: རང་ "self", རང་རྒྱལ་ "pratyekabuddha". The letter ལ is the l of "light": ལམ་ "path", ལག་པ་ "hand". The letter ཤ is the sh of "ship", with a high tone: ཤིག་ "a, an", ཤེས་རབ་ "wisdom". The letter ས is an ordinary s, high: ས་ "earth", སོགས་ "and so forth".

In shape ར falls into the same difficult group as ད and ང, and ལ resembles ར; ཤ is confused with ཨ and ཧ from the last row, and ས with མ. As before, comparing in pairs helps: ར and ད, ར and ལ, ས and མ.

Three particles with letters of this row have already appeared among the examples: ཅིག་ "a, an", ཞིག་ "a, an" and ཤིག་ "a, an" are one and the same particle in three spellings, and the last letter of the syllable before it decides which one to use. That rule belongs to the first lesson, and it is a good example of why a reader needs to see every letter.

## alphabet/8

The last row is short, two letters: ཧ ha and ཨ a, named a-chen, "big a". Both are high. With them the alphabet is complete: thirty letters in seven and a half rows. Tibetans themselves call the alphabet ka-kha, after its first two letters, much as English speaks of the ABC.

The letter ཧ is the h of "hat", the same light breath as in English. In the vocabulary of the course it stands most often under a superscribed ལ: ལྷ་ "god", ལྷ་མོ་ "goddess".

The letter ཨ carries a lone vowel. When a syllable begins not with a consonant but with a vowel sound, that vowel needs something to be written on, and ཨ is there for that: by itself it reads as a with a high tone, and with a vowel sign it gives i, u, e, o: ཨ་མ་ "mother", ཨ་རི་ "America". English simply writes a vowel letter at the start of a word; Tibetan writes a carrier and puts the vowel on it. So this vowel letter has a high tone, while a-chung from the sixth row has a low one; that is the difference between ཨ and འ.

Now it is useful to see all thirty letters together. High: ཀ ཁ ཅ ཆ ཏ ཐ པ ཕ ཙ ཚ ཤ ས ཧ ཨ, fourteen letters. Low: ག ང ཇ ཉ ད ན བ མ ཛ ཝ ཞ ཟ འ ཡ ར ལ, sixteen. The rule for the full rows is known: the first two are high, the last two low; for the remaining eight letters the tone has to be remembered, and the cards will keep showing it.

From here the course moves from letters to the syllable: to the vowel signs, to the letters written under and above others, and to prefixes and suffixes. Reading is put together from those parts.

Besides the thirty letters the script has eleven signs for Sanskrit words: six reversed letters, ཊ ཋ ཌ ཎ ཥ and ཀྵ, which write the retroflex sounds of mantras and names, and five "heavy" letters, གྷ ཛྷ ཌྷ བྷ དྷ, a letter with ཧ below for the voiced aspirates. In Tibetan speech they are read like the plain ཏ ཐ ད ན ཤ and ག ཛ ད བ ད. The course does not drill them, but their cards with recordings are in this part, so that they can be recognised in mantras.

## alphabet/9

In English the vowels are letters in the line, just like the consonants. Tibetan does it differently: the vowel a is already contained in every letter, and the other four vowels are shown by small signs placed above or below the letter. No sign means a.

There are four signs. Gi-gu above the letter gives i: ཨི, ཀི ki, the i of "machine". Zhabs-kyu below the letter gives u: ཨུ, ཀུ ku, the u of "rule". ’Greng-bu above the letter gives e: ཨེ, ཀེ ke, the e of "bed", a little closed. Na-ro above the letter gives o: ཨོ, ཀོ ko, the o of "more". In Wylie the sign is written as a letter after the consonant, so ཀི is ki and ཆོས is chos. The script has no other vowels, no long ones and no diphthongs; the ö and ü that are heard in speech appear later, under the influence of suffixes, and the part on prefixes and suffixes explains them. Unlike English, where one vowel letter can be read several ways, each sign here has one sound.

Three of the four signs stand above, and they must be told apart: gi-gu is a curl, ’greng-bu a short straight stroke, na-ro a wavy stroke. The only sign below, zhabs-kyu, cannot be confused with anything. On the cards the course shows the signs on the letter ཨ, because it adds no sound of its own, but a sign can stand on any letter.

Course words with each sign: འདི་ "this" and ཀྱི་ "of" with i; སྐུ་ "body" and གསུམ་ "three" with u; དེ་ "that" and མེ་ "fire" with e; ཆོས་ "Dharma" and མཆོག་ "supreme" with o.

The vowel sign matters for more than the sound. It always stands on the root letter of the syllable, and when a syllable has several letters, the sign shows which of them is the main one. That becomes the chief method of reading in the part on syllables.

Tibetans read an unfamiliar spelling aloud letter by letter, and for a syllable with a vowel sign it goes: the name of the letter, the name of the sign, the syllable they make. ངོ "face" is spelled "nga, na-ro, ngo", ངུ "to cry" is "nga, zhabs-kyu, ngu". The word itself comes last and is short, one syllable; everything before it only says what the syllable is made of. The "spell it" recordings on the cards of this course follow that order, the analysis first and then the word, and the plain button plays the word alone.

## alphabet/10

A Tibetan letter can stand not only in the line but under another letter, in a small form. There are four such subjoined letters: ya, ra, la and wa. The result is a stack: the main letter on top, the small one beneath it, and the vowel sign, if there is one, belongs to the whole stack. Wylie writes a stack simply in sequence: ཀྱ kya, ཀྲ kra, ཀླ kla, ཀྭ kwa. English writing has nothing like it; the nearest idea is that two signs are read as one syllable.

The subjoined ya, ya-tak, adds a y to the consonant: ཀྱ kya, as in ཀྱི་ "of" or in བརྒྱད་ "eight". With the lip letters it changes the sound more: པྱ sounds like cha, ཕྱ like cha with a puff, བྱ like ja, མྱ like nya. So ཕྱག་རྒྱ་ "mudrā" begins with a ch sound.

The subjoined ra, ra-tak, turns the consonant into a sound made with the tongue curled back, something like the tr of "tree" or the dr of "dream": ཀྲ kra is read tra, དྲུག་ "six" is druk, སྒྲོལ་མ་ "Tārā" is drölma. The groups ཀྲ ཁྲ གྲ, ཏྲ ཐྲ དྲ and པྲ ཕྲ བྲ merge in the Lhasa pronunciation into the same three sounds: tr without a puff, tr with a puff, dr.

The subjoined la, la-tak, behaves differently: the letter above falls silent, and only la is heard, with a high tone: བླ་མ་ "guru", སློབ་མ་ "student". There is one exception: ཟླ is read da. The subjoined wa, wa-zur, a small triangle, has no effect on the sound at all: གྲྭ་པ་ "monk" is read as if it were not there.

On the cards the course shows the subjoined letters under ཀ, but they can hang under many letters. A stack is recognised by its lower part: a curl is ya, a short stroke is ra, a small la is la, a triangle is wa.

## alphabet/11

Three letters can stand not under another letter but on top of it: ra, la and sa. These are the superscribed letters, in Tibetan the "heads": ra-go, la-go, sa-go. Wylie writes the top letter first, because a stack is read from top to bottom: རྐ rka, ལྐ lka, སྐ ska. The letter beneath remains the root of the syllable, and the vowel sign, as always, belongs to the whole stack.

In the Lhasa pronunciation the superscribed letter is silent, but it leaves a trace. A low letter under it loses its light puff and sounds firm: རྒ is ga, not kha. A nasal under a superscribed letter takes a high tone: རྔ is nga said high. High letters do not change: རྟ་ "horse" is read ta. One stack sounds as a whole: ལྷ, lha, as in ལྷ་ "god" and ལྷ་མོ་ "goddess", because here the superscribed ལ keeps its sound.

Course words with superscribed letters: རྣམས་ "plural particle" is read nam with a high tone; སྐུ་ "body" and སྟོང་ "thousand" are read ku and tong. In རྒྱལ་མོ་ "queen" the stack has three storeys: superscribed ར, root ག, subjoined ཡ, and it is read gyäl: the suffix ལ turns a into ä.

A superscribed letter is easily taken for an independent letter in the line, especially ས above narrow letters. The mark of a stack is its height: a stack rises above its neighbours, and the top letter in it is reduced. On the cards the course shows the superscribed letters above ཀ, but they can stand above many letters.

With this part all three ways of putting letters on top of one another are known: subjoined below, superscribed above, the vowel sign on the whole stack. What remains are the letters that stand beside the root in the line: prefixes and suffixes.

## alphabet/12

A Tibetan syllable is built like a nest around its root letter. The full order is: prefix, superscribed letter, root, subjoined letter, vowel sign, suffix, second suffix. No syllable uses every place at once, but each of them can be filled. The root is the only part that must be there, and the vowel sign always stands on it. An example of a full syllable is བསྒྲུབས bsgrubs: prefix བ, superscribed ས, root ག, subjoined ར, vowel u, suffix བ, second suffix ས; it is read drup.

Five letters can be prefixes: ག ད བ མ འ. They are written before the root in the line and are silent in the Lhasa pronunciation: གསུམ་ "three" is sum, བཞི་ "four" is zhi, མདོ་ "sūtra" is do, འདི་ "this" is di. Like a superscribed letter, a prefix makes the low letter after it firm and gives a nasal a high tone: དགུ་ "nine" is gu, གཉིས་ "two" is nyi with a high tone. English is used to letters that are written but not heard, the k of "knight", the b of "comb"; Tibetan silent letters are of the same kind, only more regular, since the five prefixes are always silent.

Ten letters can be suffixes: ག ང ད ན བ མ འ ར ལ ས. They stand after the root and its vowel, and they fall into three groups by what they do to the sound. ང, མ, ར are heard as they are: དང་ "and" is dang, ལམ་ "path" is lam, འཁོར་ "retinue" is khor. ག is heard as a soft k or a catch in the voice, བ as a p or w: མཆོག་ "supreme" is chok, ཟབ་མོ་ "profound" is zapmo. The third group matters most: ད, ན, ལ, ས. ད and ས are not pronounced, ན and ལ are, but all four change the vowel before them: a becomes ä, o becomes ö, u becomes ü. The sound ä is close to the a of "cat"; ö is an e said with the lips rounded as for o, and ü an i said with the lips rounded as for u, the sounds of German or French, which English lacks. So བོད་ "Tibet" is read bö, ཆོས་ "Dharma" chö, རྒྱལ་མོ་ "queen" gyälmo, ཀུན་ "all" kün.

The suffix འ is silent and only lengthens the vowel: བཀའ་ "words" is ka. The second suffix is only ས after another suffix, and it too is silent: རྣམས་ "plural particle" is nam, སངས་རྒྱས་ "Buddha" is sang-gye.

This part is the key to reading. Everything that Tibetan writes but does not sound, the prefixes, the superscribed letters, the suffixes ད and ས, is left over from an older pronunciation, and Wylie faithfully shows that spelling, as English spelling keeps the gh of "night". So drop nothing when reading silently, and when reading aloud apply the rules of this part.

## alphabet/13

Now everything comes together: a syllable is in front of the reader, and the task is to find its root and how it sounds. The order is always the same. First look for a vowel sign: the letter under it is the root. If there is no sign, look for a stack: the letter with something above or below it is the root. If there is neither, then in a pair of letters the root is the first and the suffix the second: ཁང is read khang, ངན is ngän. In a bare run of three letters the first is a prefix, the second the root, the third a suffix: དམར is mar, གསར is sar, བཟང is zang; but if the last letter is ས and another suffix stands before it, the syllable begins with the root: སངས is sang.

The rest applies the rules of the earlier parts. Once the root is found, see whether it is low or high and whether something before it or above it makes it firm. Then read the vowel, remembering the suffixes ད ན ལ ས, which turn a, o, u into ä, ö, ü. Then add the suffix, if it is heard.

A few syllables of the course to take apart. ཆོས: root ཆ with the sign o, suffix ས: chö. སློབ: superscribed ས, root ལ, sign o, suffix བ: lop. རྙིང: superscribed ར, root ཉ with the sign i, suffix ང: nying with a high tone. མཆོག: prefix མ, root ཆ, sign o, suffix ག: chok. བདེ: prefix བ, root ད with the sign e: de. འབྱོར: prefix འ, root བ with subjoined ཡ and the sign o, suffix ར: jor. རྫོགས: superscribed ར, root ཛ, sign o, suffix ག, second suffix ས: dzok. ཕྱག: root ཕ with subjoined ཡ, suffix ག: chak. སྒྲོལ: superscribed ས, root ག, subjoined ར, sign o, suffix ལ: dröl.

In the training of this part every syllable is taken from a word of the course, so each will be met again in words and sentences: ཆོས་ "Dharma", སློབ་མ་ "student", རྙིང་པ་ "old", མཆོག་ "supreme", བདེ་བ་ "happiness", རྣལ་འབྱོར་པ་ "yogin", རྫོགས་པ་ "perfection", ཕྱག་རྒྱ་ "mudrā", སྒྲོལ་མ་ "Tārā". Taking a syllable apart is slow at first; after a couple of dozen syllables it becomes a glance.

Two lists from the textbook Basic Tibetan fix the rule about bare letters. In a pair the root is always the first: ངག "speech", ཆང "beer", ནད "illness", ཉན "to listen", ཁབ "needle", ལམ "road", ཆར "rain", བལ "wool", རས "cloth", ཟད "to be exhausted". And in the fourteen three-letter combinations ending in ས the root is the first letter too, with ས as the second suffix: གངས "snow", ནགས "forest", ནངས "next morning", ཁམས "the Kham region", བགས "gradually", བངས "to get soaked", བབས "to descend", ལགས "polite particle", ལངས "to get up", རགས "to obtain", རངས "to be delighted", རབས "generation", རམས "indigo", སངས "to clear away".

## alphabet/14

A Tibetan word is one or more syllables separated by the tsheg. Most words of the course have two syllables, and very often the second one is one of the particles པ, བ, མ, པོ, བོ, མོ: ཁང་པ་ "house", དགོན་པ་ "monastery", སློབ་མ་ "student", དམར་པོ་ "red", ཆེན་པོ་ "big", ལྷ་མོ་ "goddess". So when a syllable is followed by a bare syllable of one letter and a sign, that is almost always the end of a word.

The tsheg stands after every syllable, not between words, so by itself it does not show a word boundary. ཉི་མ་ "sun" and སངས་རྒྱས་ "Buddha" are one word each, though they contain as many dots as two one-syllable words in a row. English marks every word with a space; a Tibetan reader finds the boundaries by knowing the words, and that is why the course teaches reading words as wholes before it turns to sentences. In Wylie the syllables are separated by a space, and the word gön-pa looks like dgon pa; that is syllable by syllable, not two words.

Long words are often put together from short ones: རྣལ་འབྱོར་པ་ "yogin" is རྣལ་འབྱོར་ "yoga" with the particle པ; དཀྱིལ་འཁོར་ "maṇḍala" is "centre" and "surroundings". The longest word of this part, མྱ་ངན་ལས་འདས་པ་ "nirvāṇa", literally "passed beyond sorrow", has five syllables. Read such words syllable by syllable, using the analysis of the previous part, and then put them together.

The pronunciation of a whole word has one more feature: the rules of the suffixes work inside each syllable separately. ཐམས་ཅད་ "all" is read thamchä: in the first syllable the suffix མ is heard and the second suffix ས is silent, in the second syllable ད turns a into ä. བུམ་པ་ "vase" is bumpa, ཚ་པོ་ "hot" is tsapo, བླ་མ་ "guru" is lama.

The training of this part shows the words of the course whole. If a word reads at first sight, good; if not, find the root in each syllable and build it up step by step, there is no hurry.

## alphabet/15

The last part of the alphabet module brings the first sentences of the course. A sentence, like a word, runs as an unbroken chain of syllables with tshegs; the end of a sentence or of one of its parts is marked by the shad, the vertical stroke །. Before the shad the tsheg is usually dropped, and only after the letter ང is it kept. A double shad closes a large section of a text. Tibetan writing has no capitals, no full stops, commas or question marks: everything they do in English is done by particles and by word order.

The five sentences of this part come from the first lesson. They are short noun phrases: དགོན་པ་དམར་པོ་ "red monastery", ཆོས་པ་བཟང་པོ་ "excellent Dharma practitioner", བདེ་བ་ཆེན་པོ་ "great bliss", ཉི་མ་ཚ་པོ་ "hot sun", སློབ་མ་བཟང་པོ་ "good student". Each has two words, and it is easy to see that the order is the reverse of English: first the noun, then the adjective. This is the first rule of the first lesson, where it is explained in detail; here it is enough to notice it.

When reading aloud, apply what the earlier parts put together: find the root of every syllable, watch for the suffixes ད ན ལ ས that change the vowel, remember the silent prefixes. For example, དགོན་པ་དམར་པོ་ "red monastery" is read gönpa marpo: in the first syllable the prefix ད is silent and the suffix ན turns o into ö; in the third syllable the prefix ད is silent again. The word བཟང་པོ་ "good" is read zangpo, and ཆོས་པ་ "Dharma practitioner" is chöpa.

The course shows every sentence with its Wylie and its translation, and every word can be tapped. The training of this part is to choose the meaning of a sentence, and the check is to build it from tiles, word by word. From this point the lessons begin: grammar is added to reading, and the sentences grow.
